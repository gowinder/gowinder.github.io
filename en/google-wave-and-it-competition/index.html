Thoughts on IT Competition, Inspired by Google Wave

Author
 2010-05-09  994 words   5 minutes    views  
Contents

Today I took my wife out for DQ, and then while keeping her company over a bowl of Ajisen ramen I drank some iced lemon water. The result was that the moment I got home I made a beeline for the toilet. Of course I didn’t forget to bring a magazine — we have a pile of back issues at home, so I grabbed a copy of CBN Weekly at random, no idea which issue it was. Flipping through it, being an IT guy, IT is naturally what interests me most, so the article about Google Wave was the first thing I read.

I’d heard the name before, but only today did I find out what Wave actually is: it turns out to be a thing that combines chat and email. My first thought was that Google always has something new, but on reflection — this stuff has existed at MSN for ages. You chat with your contacts directly, and you can also send them mail. QQ had it later too. So what’s new here? Wave was still in closed beta, though, so we’ll have to wait and actually use it before judging. But that line of thinking led me on to the competition between Microsoft and Google. You could say the cultures of these two companies are completely different, and as far as software — or rather the internet — goes, I reckon it’s these two companies fighting it out. Microsoft has put out a Wave too; Microsoft really has no capacity for innovation. Apart from DOS, everything else seems to be: wait for some small company to innovate, and once it’s about to take off, buy it outright. That’s how Microsoft grew step by step. The method worked back in an era when software companies hadn’t yet formed this big a market, but now, facing a new market like the internet and a powerful rival like Google, that trick is useless. Every move Microsoft makes is aimed at Google — search engines, chat tools, and so on — while Google seems never to have treated Microsoft as a rival at all.

Who wins this competition? Sometimes I wonder. Of course I’d like it to be Microsoft, since my own career is tied fairly closely to them, haha! But otherwise I’m not optimistic about Microsoft. Why? Even though Microsoft is currently unbeatable in operating systems (Win7 is just too awesome — once you use it you don’t want to give it up; I once switched back to XP in a fit of temper because PES 2010 was a bit laggy, and after the New Year I missed Win7 again, haha, so I set up a dual boot; I imagine plenty of people are like me), that’s still only that one field. Microsoft lacks the spirit of innovation — just look at Windows Mobile. From CE to 2003 to 6.0 there’s basically no change at all; apart from the home screen, the backend interface has barely changed. I used to be quite bullish on it in the smartphone market, and what happened? Google’s Android came from behind and overtook it in no time. Their interface is simply better looking.

Google puts more emphasis on the actual user experience, while Microsoft only talks about doing so. Fortunately, development only comes from competition: Microsoft brought out Windows Phone 7, and it’s sweet. It hasn’t officially launched yet, but the demos alone made me pretty happy. After all, on the Microsoft platform, .NET and C++ are reliable and efficient programming tools, whereas other platforms prefer to use Java.

Even so, I still think Microsoft is on the way down. Why do I always think that? Because in the internet space, the fight between the big companies is really a fight between corporate cultures — put more bluntly, a fight for talent. Whichever company’s culture can attract emerging talent better will have the last laugh. Look around the world, or look through history: which country ever became strong for any reason other than its people? Look at Microsoft’s culture — I hear that internally Microsoft won’t even let employees use an iPhone. I don’t think the iPhone is anything special, but there’s no need to ban employees from using one. After all, young guys in IT all like new things, and there’s always someone who likes weird things, so it’s hardly surprising that plenty of them like the iPhone that everyone likes. Never mind that WM isn’t up to much — even if your WM were brilliant, you still couldn’t behave like that. The more you push, the more resistance you get; maybe the person who defeats Microsoft in the future will come from among Microsoft’s own suppressed employees (there are plenty of such examples in history). Google, by contrast, is more open, which makes it easier to attract talent. Job-hopping in the IT industry carries far less risk than defecting on an ancient battlefield (no need to worry about your family being avenged, haha). If Microsoft keeps posturing as the big boss like this, there’s a very good chance it will be beaten by a company with an open culture and an innovative spirit like Google. Microsoft makes money off programmers; Google lets programmers make money alongside it (a pity I don’t know Java and don’t plan to learn it, otherwise what a wonderful thing that would be).

Looking at the Microsoft–Google rivalry, and then looking at the companies around me — sure, they can’t be compared with Google and Microsoft for size, but as things stand the gap isn’t a small one. The so-called culture at companies like that is: work overtime to ship a product stuffed with features that bare their fangs and grab money from users, then have a quick thrill and die. Companies like that have no vision, they only care about immediate profit, and in the end all they achieve is a drain of talent before they vanish from the competitive landscape.
